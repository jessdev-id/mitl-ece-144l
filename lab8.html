Radio Frequencies and Channels

Wireless communication uses radio frequencies instead of cables. A range of frequencies is called a band . Chapter 8 emphasizes the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands. Each band is divided into channels.

2.4 GHz

Some channels overlap. For a common three-channel deployment, use channels 1, 6, and 11 to reduce adjacent-channel interference.

5 GHz

The textbook contrasts 5 GHz with 2.4 GHz because the standard 20 MHz channel plan can provide nonoverlapping channels.

Engineering note: channel width, local regulatory rules, and available channels matter in real deployments. The chapter's 5 GHz discussion is a simplified introduction.

IEEE 802.11 Standards

The textbook's Table 8.1 compares these standards. The listed speeds are theoretical maximums, not guaranteed application throughput.

Standard Year Band Textbook maximum
802.11 1997 2.4 GHz 2 Mbps
802.11b 1999 2.4 GHz 11 Mbps
802.11a 1999 5 GHz 54 Mbps
802.11g 2003 2.4 GHz 54 Mbps
802.11n 2009 Both 600 Mbps
802.11ac 2013 5 GHz 6.93 Gbps
802.11ax 2019 Both Up to 30% faster than 802.11ac (book wording)

Service Set Identifier (SSID)

The SSID identifies a wireless network. Access points typically broadcast the SSID so clients can discover a network name. A hidden SSID is not displayed in the normal scan list, so users may need to enter its exact name manually.

Does hiding the SSID secure a network?

No. Chapter 8 describes hiding SSID broadcast as network cloaking but emphasizes that it does not provide meaningful security by itself. Proper wireless encryption is necessary.
